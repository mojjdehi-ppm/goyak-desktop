## Key Question 1: AVL data requirements for headway control (sampling interval, accuracy, latency, 60-s/irregular reporting, stop-arrival detection, missing vehicles)

### Takeaway
Field headway-control deployments have run on 15-s GPS (Georgia Tech) and on agency arrival predictions (CTA) rather than raw high-rate telemetry; legacy CAD/AVL polls every 60–120 s, which is workable for control-point holding only if stop events are inferred with a map-matched resampling method (errors ~5–10 s on stop duration) and if the follower bus is actually tracked. Untracked ("missing") vehicles break any backward-looking rule and must trigger a fallback.

### Cited Findings
- Georgia Tech field test (2011–2012, FIELD) relied on NextBus, "which collects the positions of buses from their GPS devices every 15 s"; departure times were initially computed by a student at each control point, later by a tablet-and-server system — [Bartholdi & Eisenstein, TR-B 2012 (preprint PDF)](https://www2.isye.gatech.edu/~jjb/papers/Bartholdi-Eisenstein-2011.pdf)
- "For the state-of-the-art CAD/AVL systems, the polling interval to get a transit vehicle's location update is generally between 60 and 120 seconds (TRB, 2008)"; in a field comparison, 90-s and 1-s location data gave no significant ETA accuracy difference more than 5 min before arrival, but 1-s data was better in the window from 5 min before arrival to 3 min after departure — [Meng et al., ITSIS field test, UC PATH 2017](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1dm2q3hf.pdf;origin=repeccitec)
- Across surveyed US agencies, "location information on each vehicle is updated… with a frequency of 30 s to 5 min"; London Buses removed a route from Countdown signs if AVL performance dropped below 65% (e.g., driver not logged on) — [TCRP Synthesis 48](https://onlinepubs.trb.org/onlinepubs/tcrp/tcrp_syn_48c3.pdf)
- Blacksburg AVL study (FIELD): minimum sample interval 30 s, average 46 s, ~15% of records >1 min apart, longest gap ~7 min; at 25 km/h and 30-s polling the bus position is unknown over a segment up to ~200 m; 90% of GPS points fell within 82.5 m of the route link, points >100 m off were discarded as outliers — [Lin & Zeng, ROSA P](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/5137/dot_5137_DS1.pdf)
- Round-robin polling ("location-at-time") data "introduces interpolation error" and "imprecise matching, particularly in periods of traffic congestion, when it can be difficult to distinguish whether a bus is… in a queue waiting to reach the stop, is at the stop with its doors open, or has left the stop"; the survey "did not find any transit agency routinely using Level A [polling] data for operation analyses"; OC Transpo logs an "idle time" event when a bus has not moved for 45 s — [Furth et al., 2004, Designing AVL for archived data analysis](http://www1.coe.neu.edu/~pfurth/Furth%20papers/2004%20designing%20AVL%20for%20archived%20analysis.pdf)
- Boston Route 1 (FIELD, 60-s NextBus data): a stop is defined as a window of ±15 m (2G = 30 m) around the stop location along the route shape; a model-based resampling validated against 1-s GPS gave a mean absolute deviation in stop duration of 4.83 s (vs 7.7 s for linear interpolation) in one direction and 6.5 s (vs 7.7 s) in the other; the authors conclude data "with a sampling rate as low as 1 min, when processed appropriately, can provide ample information"; data quality problems included wrong temporal order, points far from the shape (esp. at trip start/end), and trips with only a few irregular points indicating out-of-service or broken GPS — [Yang, Gerstle, Widhalm, Bauer, González, TRR](https://humnetlab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/TRRecordsBuses.pdf)
- Swiftly (VENDOR, via agency board memo): live vehicle tracking "updates every 5–10 seconds"; adding router GPS alongside the Clever Devices BusTime API gave "40% more stop-level data" and recovered 7% of data otherwise discarded for missing operator login — [County Connection board memo, 2024](https://countyconnection.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/7.b.1.-Swiftly-Modules.pdf)
- Swiftly (VENDOR blog): traditional MDTs "suffer from poor AVL ping rates (up 6 minutes between pings)… vehicle operators are commonly instructed not to look at the devices" — [Swiftly, Aug 2020](https://www.goswift.ly/blog/introducing-swiftlys-onboard-app)
- Rural AVL stop-event audit (FIELD): data capture at trip terminals unreliable; one stop recorded nothing; 480 records with negative dwell; late Friday trips had 86–94% missing values; system "does not seem to record values after 1500 seconds (25 minutes) late" — [AESS Web, AVL completeness](https://archive.aessweb.com/index.php/5004/article/download/3960/6234)
- Smartphone-GPS stop extraction (FIELD): 85–100% recall but only ~50% precision because "intersections, traffic signals, traffic congestion, and stop signs can easily be confused with true bus stops" — [Biagioni et al., EasyTracker, SenSys 2011](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/2070942.2070950)
- Reconstructing stop-crossing times from noisy ETA snapshots (Singapore, FIELD): >50% precision/recall to the exact minute, >80% within ≤1 min — [arXiv 1907.08483](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.08483)
- Door-sensor stop events are not clean either: APC door-status indicators "remained continuously in an OPEN state for extended periods despite normal vehicle movement," requiring edge (state-transition) detection to avoid false stop events — [Economy Informatics, hybrid AVL/APC validation](https://economyinformatics.ase.ro/ojs/index.php/eij/article/download/19/4/74)
- Interline's stop-observation schema codes arrival `source` as 0 manual / 1 door sensor / 2 estimated from GPS-AVL and lists error sources: GPS error, run misassignment, CAD/AVL imprecision, GTFS-RT emitter issues — [Interline, 2022](https://www.interline.io/blog/mtc-regional-stop-observations/)
- CTA pilots (FIELD): inputs were GTFS, historical APC and real-time arrival predictions at control stops; holding was discretised in 30-s steps; AVL ping rate and stop-detection method not stated — [Rodriguez, Koutsopoulos, Zhao, arXiv 2509.08231](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08231)
- Baku fleet coverage: World Bank (2018) counted ~2,168 buses, 41 operators; BakuBus and Xaliq Faiqoğlu fleets GPS- and card-equipped, while "about 40 operators manage the remaining fleet of about 1,500 vehicles… they accept only cash and cannot be tracked" — [World Bank Baku Urban Mobility Policy Note, June 2018](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/963521580136216965-0080022020/original/BakuUrbanMobilityPolicyNoteJune2018.pdf); AYNA says 2,160 buses operated daily in Baku in 2024, ~2,000 connected to cashless payment (+43% vs 2023), target 2,800 — [Baku.ws, 16 Apr 2025](https://baku.ws/social/ayna-bu-il-ozel-dasiyicilar-bakiya-100-den-cox-avtobus-getirecek); 27 operators serve Baku, 5 of them own 58% of the fleet (Sept 2025) — [Banker.az, 22 Sep 2025](https://banker.az/ayna-paytaxtda-avtobuslarin-orta-yasi-7-12-il-arasinda-d%c9%99yisir/); AYNA reports 68.5% of passenger vehicles under its management GPS-equipped and monitored by its Monitoring Centre (June 2025; stated in an intercity-route context) — [AYNA, 20 Jun 2025](https://ayna.gov.az/az/news/seherlerarasi-sernisindasimada-avtobus-parki-yenilenir-ve-reqemsal-heller-tetbiq-olunur-759); the AYNA Passenger Transport Monitoring Center (Dec 2022) tracks intercity buses by GPS for timetable compliance, deviations, harsh braking and speeding — [Mincom, 2 Dec 2022](https://mincom.gov.az/en/media-en/news/ayna-passenger-transport-monitoring-center-launched1704)
- BakuBus (state operator, AZCON Holding): 1,337 buses, 83 routes, 557k+ daily passengers (2025) — [AZCON](https://azcon.gov.az/en/portfolio/bakubus-4)

### Inferences
- For control-point holding the decision needs the follower's predicted arrival at the control point, not centimetre positioning; a 30-s headway error at α≈0.5 becomes a 15-s holding error, within the 30-s step CTA used. So 15–30-s pings are comfortable, 60-s pings are workable with resampling, and irregular >2-min gaps should disable control for that pair of buses.
- A realistic latency budget (inference from the sources above): GPS fix → cellular upload (1–5 s) → server (1–3 s) → tablet push (1–3 s) is ~10 s for a Swiftly-style stack, versus 60–120 s for legacy polling; because the hold must be shown as the bus arrives, the hold should be computed from the forecast follower headway before arrival and refreshed during dwell.
- Stop arrival should be inferred from a geofence of roughly ±15–30 m along the route shape plus a speed/stationarity rule (as in Yang et al. and OC Transpo's 45-s idle rule), and validated against a few days of door-open or manual observations; expect ±5–10 s with good resampling and far worse with naive interpolation.
- Missing vehicles are the dominant Baku-specific risk: with a meaningful fraction of buses on interval-based ETAs, a backward-looking rule cannot compute a hold. The MVP must detect "follower untracked" and fall back to forward-headway or schedule-based holding, and display the data-quality state to the dispatcher.

### Gaps
- No public figure found for the share of Baku city buses with real-time GPS in 2026 (the 68.5% figure is AYNA-wide/intercity-framed).
- No published GPS ping interval or positional accuracy for AYNA/BakuBus telematics.
- No source quantifies the error rate of geofence-only stop detection versus door sensors for a 60-s feed in seconds per event beyond the Boston Route 1 numbers.

## Key Question 2: Integrating with an existing driver console/validator (CAD/AVL APIs, MDT, SIRI/GTFS-RT, ITxPT); can a third party add a screen message without the fare-system vendor; cost and timeline examples

### Takeaway
GTFS-RT and SIRI are passenger-information standards, not channels to the driver; in-cab messaging goes through the CAD/AVL vendor's MDT or, under ITxPT, the MADT driver-terminal service. The two routes that avoid the fare/CAD vendor entirely are (a) a separate commodity tablet app (the Swiftly Onboard model, now used by 50+ US agencies) and (b) a human relay via dispatcher/supervisor, which is exactly what CTA had to do because supervisors had no direct line to drivers.

### Cited Findings
- CTA (FIELD, 2022–23): "The supervisors at CTA do not have a direct communication line with bus drivers; therefore, the protocol was for the supervisors to physically meet the drivers at the stop." For scaling, the authors see "potential in using automated driver instructions via the on-board digital device" and note the radio channel "can also be used for control instructions," adding upfront cost and driver re-training — [Rodriguez et al., arXiv 2509.08231](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08231)
- Swiftly Onboard App (VENDOR): "requires no integrations with other onboard systems and is completely hardware-agnostic"; uses the tablet's own GPS; "a tablet plus Onboard App costs about one-tenth the price of a typical MDT"; traditional MDTs "cost thousands of dollars per vehicle"; displays bunching/gapping status for headway-based service — [Swiftly, Aug 2020](https://www.goswift.ly/blog/introducing-swiftlys-onboard-app); "More than 50 transit agencies" use it; it "runs on a ruggedized commodity tablet installed alongside an agency's existing CAD/AVL system"; Denton County (DCTA) reported +18.6% on-time performance and −66% early departures (VENDOR case study) — [Swiftly, Jun 2026](https://www.goswift.ly/blog/onboard-app-reliable-service); a separate Swiftly post gives −55% early departures for DCTA — [Swiftly, Sep 2025](https://www.goswift.ly/blog/dcta-otp-case-study)
- MDOT MTA (agency figures via trade press): a 15-day pilot on one division in fall 2025 found buses with the Onboard App "25% more likely to be on time and… 37% less likely to be early"; wider rollout planned by summer 2026 — [Mass Transit, 6 Mar 2026](https://www.masstransitmag.com/technology/news/55362287/mdot-mta-aims-to-improve-on-time-performance-by-empowering-bus-operators-with-real-time-information)
- CapMetro quote (VENDOR-published): "They know how far away the next bus is—ahead of them, behind them… the countdown is telling them when they should leave that timepoint or stop" — [Swiftly, Jun 2026](https://www.goswift.ly/blog/onboard-app-reliable-service)
- Pricing datapoint: County Connection (CA) paid $181,000 for one year of three Swiftly modules (Live Operations/Predictions, GPS Playback, Service Adjustments), FY2025 — [County Connection memo](https://countyconnection.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/7.b.1.-Swiftly-Modules.pdf)
- ITxPT services currently specified include Module Inventory, Time, GNSS Location, FMStoIP, VEHICLEtoIP, AVMS, APC, MADT (driver terminal) and MQTT Broker; specifications are free but require sign-up; the architecture builds on SIRI, NeTEx, Transmodel, TS13149 — [ITxPT Specifications](https://itxpt.org/specifications/); ITxPT says a bus can be delivered with "a Gateway, an FMS to IP bridge and a MADT driver terminal" and an AVMS added later and integrated — [ITxPT FAQ](https://itxpt.org/ufaq/which-systems-are-involved-in-the-itxpt-architecture/)
- Typical MDT messaging functions (VENDOR bid): dispatcher sends freeform or predefined messages to drivers' MDTs; drivers respond from a pick-list; agencies can disable message viewing while the vehicle is in motion — [Strategic Mapping bid to Topeka Metro](https://www.topekametro.org/uploads/f1gTZD0n/Bid-StrategicMappingInc.forPosting.pdf)
- Fare system ↔ CAD/AVL integrations exist but are vendor-to-vendor (VENDOR): Masabi lists in-vehicle or back-office integrations with Vontas, Trapeze, Conduent, Avail, Equans, Clever Devices and Init, "including support for ITxPT integrations" — [Masabi blog, May 2023](https://blog.masabi.com/blog/myth-busted-because-we-want-to-integrate-cad/avl-we-have-to-buy-bespoke-fare-collection-systems)
- Even a small cross-agency change (showing NORTA predictions on Jefferson Parish BusTime signs) required a Clever Devices scope of work with vendor software development, testing and a confidential quotation (June 2023) — [Clever Devices SOW via NORTA Legistar](https://norta.legistar.com/View.ashx?GUID=939DD31E-AB29-4044-A0D7-017D5D5A3483&ID=12281911&M=F)
- Standards usage: Swiftly outputs predictions "in the industry-standard GTFS-rt format" to passenger endpoints and sends instructions to operators only via its own Onboard App — [Carahsoft/Swiftly](https://www.carahsoft.com/swiftly/solutions); display vendors ingest GTFS-RT and SIRI for passenger signage — [MessagePoint Media](https://www.mpmedia.tv/platform/); Vontas–Swiftly–Transit share service disruptions via APIs — [Vontas](https://vontas.com/solutions/integration-and-data-sharing/)
- Baku: AYNA MaaS declares "No data shared with third parties" on Google Play — [Google Play listing](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?hl=en_US&id=com.aynamaas.app); the BakıKart system in 2018 was "administered by a private company, which does the clearing at the percentage of the cost of the fares collected" — [World Bank 2018](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/963521580136216965-0080022020/original/BakuUrbanMobilityPolicyNoteJune2018.pdf)

### Inferences
- A third party cannot push text to a BakıKart validator or a vendor MDT without that vendor's cooperation; contractually this is AYNA's/operators' decision and technically a vendor change order. The timeline and cost of the Clever Devices and Swiftly examples (months; five- to six-figure USD) are out of reach for a student pilot.
- The credible pilot path is a sideloaded Android tablet/phone app in a handful of buses (Swiftly model) or a dispatcher-relayed instruction via AYNA's Monitoring Centre radio/phone (CTA model). Both avoid the fare vendor and both are defensible to judges if stated plainly.
- Pitch language matters: "we integrate with the driver console" is a red flag to experts; "we run on a commodity tablet alongside existing systems and can later map to ITxPT MADT" is defensible.

### Gaps
- Could not identify AYNA's/BakuBus's CAD/AVL or validator vendors or whether any ITxPT-compliant equipment exists in Baku.
- No published cost/timeline for an MDT message-integration project specifically (only the generic SOW and Swiftly pricing above).

## Key Question 3: Control-logic credibility (best simple headway rule, typical parameters, measured gap between rules and ML/RL, the CTA pilot, answering "where is the AI?")

### Takeaway
The literature's "best simple rule" is a linear holding law using forward and/or backward headway (Daganzo 2009; Daganzo–Pilachowski 2011; Xuan–Argote–Daganzo 2011's one-parameter near-optimal control; Bartholdi–Eisenstein's backward-only rule with α≈0.5), which cuts required slack ~40% versus schedule holding and has worked in the field. RL beats these rules only in simulation (typically by tens of seconds of waiting time); the one RL field deployment (CTA 2022–23) wrapped the model in rule-based guardrails and an even-headway fallback and achieved 5.7–8.7% wait-time reductions with 35–57% driver compliance.

### Cited Findings
- Daganzo (2009, TR-B 43(10):913–921): a dynamic headway-based holding scheme "requires less slack than the conventional, schedule-based approach to produce headways within a given tolerance," raising commercial speed — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transb/v43y2009i10p913-921.html)
- Xuan, Argote & Daganzo (2011, TR-B): a one-parameter "simple" control is near-optimal; "To put it in practice, one only needs the arrival times of the current bus and the preceding bus relative to the virtual schedule"; it "was found to require about 40% less slack than the conventional schedule-based method" — [TRID](https://trid.trb.org/View/1124648); [eScholarship preprint](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0jp7c8k8/qt0jp7c8k8.pdf)
- Formulas (Argote, Xuan & Gayah 2012, CASPT): forward headway control D = d_s − (α+β_s)(ε_n,s − ε_{n−1,s}) with 0<α<1; backward (Bartholdi–Eisenstein) D = d_s + α(ε_{n+1,s} − ε_n,s); two-way (Eulerian Daganzo–Pilachowski) D = d_s + [α ε_{n+1,s} − (1−2α) ε_n,s + α ε_{n−1,s}] with 0<α<1/2; simple (Xuan) f0 = α. In a Bear Transit simulation (SIMULATION, field-measured demand and travel times): no control gave commercial speed 11.42 km/h but headway SD 361.7 s and 34.1% bunching; schedule-based gave 7.08 km/h, 37.4% holding time, SD 29.2 s; two-way 9.27 km/h, 19.0% holding, SD 44.1 s; simple 9.31 km/h, 18.8% holding, SD 47.9 s, 95.6% on-time; the authors caution "these strategies have not been tested under realistic conditions" and "the best way to compare these control strategies is to test them in the field" — [Argote et al. 2012](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~xuanyg/doc/Argote_Xuan_Gayah_2012_CASPT12.pdf)
- Bartholdi & Eisenstein (2012, TR-B; FIELD): Georgia Tech route, 3.3 mi, ~5,000 riders/day, 6 buses, target 6-min headway; parameters α1=α2=7/12≈0.5 and β=5 min at two control points (simulation search later found α=0.55 best); results: smallest headway 2 min 28 s vs 1 s under schedule; largest 14 min 59 s vs 19 min 45 s; after removing one of three buses, headways re-equalised at ~17 min without management action; drivers "liked not having to check the clock constantly" — [Bartholdi & Eisenstein PDF](https://www2.isye.gatech.edu/~jjb/papers/Bartholdi-Eisenstein-2011.pdf); a student-built tablet/server system ran "live trials for over one year," public demo 20 Apr 2012, "full-time use at Georgia Tech in August 2013" — [GT project page](https://www.isye.gatech.edu/~jjb/buses/buses.html); Xuan et al. note the method "has been tested successfully with an experiment for a case with low demand" — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0jp7c8k8/qt0jp7c8k8.pdf)
- CTA pilots (FIELD; Rodriguez, Koutsopoulos, Zhao 2025): DQN (PyTorch) trained in an event-based Gymnasium simulator with travel times from empirical distributions and APC/AFC-based demand; non-compliance in training modelled as executed hold uniformly 50–100% of instruction; guardrails: lateness threshold s^l = 5 min (hold zeroed if departure would exceed schedule + 5 min), early-departure limit s^e = 4 min at the route 81 terminal, holds in 30-s steps; even-headway fallback π = max((Ĥ_{i+1,j} − H_{i,j})/2, 0). Route 81 (17–21 Oct 2022, >8,000 riders/weekday, 15–25% of trips cancelled): 158 trips, 80 (50.6%) with adjusted departures, 28 compliant within 45 s → 35% compliance; AM wait −8.7% (0.6 min/rider), PM 1.6 min/rider saved. Route 66 (6–17 Nov 2023, ~15,000 rides/weekday, holding at three rail-transfer timepoints): 174 instructions, 99 compliant → 56.9%; average wait −5.7% (0.3 min); 90th-percentile load 60.0 → 56.7 riders; achieved with 1.6 fewer trips/day than baseline. Compliance fell the further the instruction deviated from schedule; non-compliant trips were driven by more senior drivers; a control stop just before a signal lowered compliance; no quantified sim-vs-field gap reported — [arXiv 2509.08231](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08231)
- Cooperative holding + stop-skipping MARL (SIMULATION, Chicago route): robust at compliance lower bounds of 0.8 and 0.6; authors assume drivers depart early but never later than instructed — [Rodriguez et al., TR-C 2023 (OSTI)](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2577182)
- Phillips et al. (2015, TR-A 78:463–472; SIMULATION with Delgado et al. 2012 model): "In the majority of the cases the benefits of these models have been estimated assuming ideal operational conditions"; non-compliance concentrated in some drivers "deters the level of service more than if it is spread homogeneously across drivers" — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856415001639)
- Review (Gkiotsalitis & Cats 2021, TR-E): "cooperative fleet control strategies such as even-headway holding… are highly robust to the impacts of imperfect driver compliance (Cats et al., 2012; Phillips et al., 2015) as well as imprecise communication with the driver display (Cats et al., 2012)" — [doi:10.1016/j.tre.2020.102176](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tre.2020.102176)
- Stockholm (Cats et al., CTS 2013; AVL analysis + BusMezzo SIMULATION): schedule-based holding at a few timepoints "is not effective in improving service regularity"; drivers "adjust their speed based on performance objectives"; two-way holding at all stops improves performance considerably; practical difficulties listed: terminal dispatching, driver scheduling, compliance — [CTS 2013:26](https://www.transportportal.se/swopec/CTS2013-26.pdf)
- TU Delft thesis on R-net 400 (SIMULATION): headway-based strategies are "less sensitive to (non-)compliance of drivers. Non-complying drivers are presumably corrected by the other drivers" — [TU Delft repository](http://resolver.tudelft.nl/uuid:d5d3d495-439c-49b1-bd57-f45c46041d12)
- Santiago driver survey (FIELD): experienced drivers (>50) "appear to be more reluctant to use onboard headway control tools"; less experienced drivers say it improved their driving — [Martínez-Estupiñán et al., Transportmetrica A 2023, via TR-A citing list](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856415001639)
- RL vs rules (SIMULATION): Wang & Sun 2020 (TR-C) used 12 stops, 6 buses, max holding 180 s, holds <30 s ignored, boarding 3.0 s/pax, alighting 1.8 s/pax; MARL imposed less holding and lower waiting than forward/backward headway rules, which themselves kept waiting time stable while schedule/threshold holding let it grow — [Wang & Sun 2020 PDF](https://lijunsun.github.io/files/papers/2020-TRC-Bus.pdf); Wang & Sun IJCAI 2021 on a real-route simulation: forward-headway rule cut average waiting by 70–87 s with 67–68 s average holds, the best RL (CAAC) cut it by 161–166 s with 40–42 s holds — [IJCAI 2021](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0060.pdf); a 2024 DRL study reports headway variation −37.98% and waiting −23.61% versus headway-based control — [TSEIT 2024](http://www.tseit.org.cn/EN/Y2024/V24/I5/173); a 2025 systematic evaluation finds "simpler state representations (e.g., considering only forward and backward spacings…) may outperform more complex ones" and discrete holding times perform comparably — [Xu et al., IEEE T-ITS 2025](https://doi.org/10.1109/tits.2025.3545642); a 2026 offline-RL benchmark concludes "no single controller was best on every passenger and service outcome" and results "are limited to the simulated setting" — [Zhang et al., TSE 2026](https://academic.oup.com/tse/advance-article/doi/10.1093/tse/tdag059/8812242)
- "Where is the AI?" as a judging question: at a 2026 government AI hackathon in Selangor, judges asked (1) does it work on records as they are, (2) "Where is the AI, and can you name it? … A panel that asks that is not impressed by a chatbot wrapper," (3) does it change a number the agency reports — [Gotchaa Lab, Sep 2026](https://gotchaa-lab.com/blog/2026-09-10-selangor-ai-hackathon-2026-top-10); a buyer's "AI Capture Test": would the product still work if the AI were removed; what does it do beyond a direct API call; does accuracy improve on your data — [rework.com, Apr 2026](https://resources.rework.com/guides/saas-buying-framework/evaluating-ai-enabled-saas)

### Inferences
- Parameters a judge will accept: two-way-looking linear rule with α≈0.5 (backward-only) or α≈0.25–0.45 (two-way, must be <0.5), control points at terminals plus 2–3 high-dwell transfer stops, a hold cap of 60–120 s (CTA stepped in 30 s and capped lateness at 5 min; Wang & Sun capped at 180 s; Georgia Tech used β=5 min at terminals), and a rule to skip holds <30 s. The hold cap values 60–120 s are a synthesis, not a single-source number.
- Honest answer to "where is the AI": the control law should be a transparent rule (that is what worked in the field); the learned components are (i) the follower's arrival-time/headway forecast feeding the backward term, (ii) dwell-time/demand prediction, (iii) optionally an RL policy evaluated in simulation with the rule as the fallback, as CTA did. Claiming an RL controller as the production decision-maker invites the CTA question "what are your guardrails and how do you know compliance?"
- Expected real-world magnitude: CTA's 5.7–8.7% wait reduction with partial compliance and Georgia Tech's elimination of sub-2.5-min headways are the right anchors; simulation gaps of 20–60% versus no control are not field evidence.

### Gaps
- No source gives a measured field gap between an RL controller and a simple rule on the same route; the CTA paper reports the RL+guardrails package, not a head-to-head.
- No hold-cap value is stated in the CTA paper beyond the 5-min lateness guard and 30-s steps.

## Key Question 4: Stop-level crowd forecasting with Baku's available data (no public tap-ins); realistic accuracy from POI + time-of-day + metro exits + weather; published MAE/MAPE; what AYNA would need to share

### Takeaway
Published stop-level demand prediction works only when historical tap-in or APC counts exist; even then stop-level models overfit badly (Seoul: 94.8% train vs 52.3% test match) and POI features add only ~10–15% over a historical average. Without BakıKart or APC data, a POI/time/weather model is a static "typical crowding profile," not a forecast, and should be presented as such; the data AYNA would need to share is stop × 15-min aggregated tap counts (anonymised) and, ideally, any APC loads.

### Cited Findings
- Seoul (smart card, DNN): the stop-level model "suffered from an overfitting of the data" — 94.76% match in training vs at most 52.3% in test — while the stop-to-stop model reached 71.94% test match; the authors call even that "unsatisfactory" — [Baek & Sohn, Applied Artificial Intelligence 2016](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/08839514.2016.1277291)
- Beijing route 966 (smart card): short-term route OD forecasting with MAPE ≤13.05% and max absolute error ≤17 passengers — [Chen et al., ITSC 2011](https://doi.org/10.1109/itsc.2011.6082929)
- Beijing XGBoost + POI (smart card, 30-min bins, 7–9 am): boarding prediction RMSE 7.84 / MAE 7.32 / R² 0.912 with POI versus 8.96 / 8.34 / 0.885 for the historical average; alighting is more predictable than boarding — [Lv et al., Applied Sciences 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/app12030940)
- Changsha (smart card): at the aggregate level, FCN predicts stop ridership but not its temporal profile, LSTM/RNN capture time but "all models lack the ability to make the prediction at the stop level"; boarding at a given stop-hour is a rare, imbalanced event — [Tang et al., 2021 preprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/175414/1/Tang%20et%20al%20-SCS2021-%20deep%20learning%20of%20bus%20boardings%20-%20WRR.pdf); [Tang et al., IEEE T-ITS 2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/tits.2023.3237134)
- Melbourne (smart card, 18 routes, 1,781 stops): BiLSTM "over 90% accuracy" at route/temporal level — [Liyanage et al., J. Urban Management 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jum.2022.05.002)
- Trondheim (APC, no tap-ins): XGBoost best; weather, terrain, demographics and land use improved results; stacking reduced RMSE from 2.82 to 2.64 with R² only 0.575 (boarding) and 0.553 (alighting) per stop-visit; "stop-level modelling lacks inductive biases on the spatial structure of transit networks" — [Yusuf, Rasheed & Lindseth, Transportation 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11116-025-10689-4)
- Campus stops with Wi-Fi probe counts + weather (no tap-ins): DNN MSE 1.15 vs 1.77 for linear regression (hourly waiting counts, 7 stops, one month); MAC randomisation adds noise and the authors flag privacy — [Arai et al., arXiv 2021](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2102.01364)
- Manual-count ridership model (EASTS 2025): XGBoost MAE 18.82, RMSE 25.51 — [JSTAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/easts/16/0/16_PP3886/_pdf/-char/en)
- Baku data reality: ~2,000 buses cashless-connected in 2024 — [Baku.ws](https://baku.ws/social/ayna-bu-il-ozel-dasiyicilar-bakiya-100-den-cox-avtobus-getirecek); Baku Metro carries ~627,000 passengers/day across 27 stations (operator-partner page) — [SUP.VC](https://sup.vc/metro-hackathon); Baku Metro's 2026 hackathon gave participants "the metro's real database" to work on — [metro.gov.az](https://metro.gov.az/az/news/4734/baki-metropoliteni-tarixinde-ilk-metro-hackathon-layihesinin-qalibleri-belli-oldu); the National AI Centre's DataRace released data only "after being cleaned of personal, confidential and strategic information" — [Trend, 28 Aug 2026](https://az.trend.az/business/it/4218556.html)

### Inferences
- With only POI, time-of-day, metro exits and weather, the honest product is a relative crowding index (low/medium/high) per stop and hour, validated by a few days of manual counts; quoting MAPE for it would be unsupported.
- Headway itself is the best available real-time proxy for load (Daganzo's β links dwell to headway), so a "crowding" layer can be derived from the headway model plus a static arrival-rate profile rather than from a separate ML model.
- Minimum data request to AYNA/operators for a pilot: anonymised BakıKart boardings aggregated by stop × 15 min × day type for one or two routes, historical AVL stop events for the same routes, and any APC loads; the Metro Hackathon and DataRace precedents show Baku institutions have released cleaned operational data under programme rules.

### Gaps
- No published stop-level demand study uses only POI/time/weather with no historical counts; accuracy for that configuration is unknown.
- No information on whether BakıKart taps are stop-tagged (validators record route/vehicle; stop attribution would need GPS fusion) — unverified.

## Key Question 5: Reserve-bus dispatch (formulation, deadhead and driver-shift constraints, validation without real standby-fleet data)

### Takeaway
The standard formulation is the vehicle rescheduling problem: a quasi-assignment/ILP over remaining trips that minimises operating plus delay cost, choosing a backup from the depot or a compatible in-service vehicle, with deadhead compatibility, maximum lateness, cancellation penalties and depot capacity, and crew constraints when integrated; solve times of 1–5 minutes are the practical target. Without real standby data, validate with synthetic disruptions calibrated to published failure rates and compare against a dispatcher heuristic.

### Cited Findings
- Li, Mirchandani & Borenstein (2007, Networks): the vehicle rescheduling problem assigns "a node representing a backup trip, or… the depot, to the passengers of the cut trip"; quasi-assignment ILP min Σ c_ij y_ij + D_k with each trip having exactly one predecessor and successor; depot treated as a trip starting at breakdown time to rule out backups that would arrive late; "a reasonable target for the computational time to reschedule should be no more than a minute or two"; parallel auction algorithms solve large instances — [doi:10.1002/net.20199](https://doi.org/10.1002/net.20199)
- Multiple-depot bus rescheduling model (PATAT 2014): cost components are operational distance, fixed cost (with penalty) for introducing a new vehicle, deviation from the original vehicle assignment, per-minute lateness bounded by L, and cancellation (priced above the trip plus deadheads); constraints include depot capacity, flow conservation and deadhead-time compatibility linearised with big-M — [Dávid & Krész, PATAT 2014](https://www.patatconference.org/patat2014/proceedings/2_6.pdf); the same author describes driver-scheduling rules (min/max shift length, breaks) and pull-out/pull-in deadheads — [Dávid PhD 2018](https://doi.org/10.14232/phd.9745)
- Column-generation rescheduling of all remaining trips after a breakdown "in less than five minutes" — [Guedes 2017](http://hdl.handle.net/10183/163730)
- Integrated vehicle and crew rescheduling under stochastic, time-dependent travel times maximising frequency/mileage while minimising crew overtime and meal-break delays, solved in a rolling horizon (tram case) — [Lai & Leung 2017](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21680566.2017.1358678)
- Nashville WeGo (agency collaboration): substitute buses "start their day at the main depot, travel to a predetermined stationing stop to wait for dispatching"; disruption forecasting models plus randomised local search choose stationing locations — [Han et al., arXiv 2403.04072](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04072); [HTML version](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04072v1)
- Real-time control option lists include "adding a reserve vehicle," deadheading, short-turning — [Chow, arXiv 2305.04324](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2305.04324v1.pdf)
- Failure-rate benchmarks for synthetic scenarios: SFMTA targets 12,000 miles mean distance between failures for motor coaches (FY22-23), computed as mileage ÷ chargeable road calls — [SFMTA](https://www.sfmta.com/reports/muni-mean-distance-between-failure); Victor Valley Transit targets fewer than 5 NTD mechanical road calls per 100,000 miles and a 20% spare ratio — [VVTA maintenance plan](https://vvta.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Exhibit-M-4-Vehicle-Maintenance-Plan.pdf); TCRP Synthesis 22 reports a 3,000-mile road-call goal (MCTS, 1996) and UPS's one road call per 300 vehicle-days — [TCRP Synthesis 22](https://onlinepubs.trb.org/onlinepubs/tcrp/tsyn22.pdf); NTD defines a major mechanical failure as one that prevents completing or starting a scheduled revenue trip — [CTPS 2022](https://www.ctps.org/data/html/programs/performance/2022-Transit-Safety-Performance-Targets.html)
- Demand-side "disruptions" can dominate: CTA route 81 had 15–25% of daily trips cancelled from absenteeism in Oct 2022 — [arXiv 2509.08231](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08231)
- Baku operator incentives: AYNA's chairman said the fare-collection business model leads carriers to "carry more passengers with minimum resources, which in turn creates problems with adherence to schedules and bus capacity utilisation" (Sept 2025) — [Banker.az](https://banker.az/ayna-paytaxtda-avtobuslarin-orta-yasi-7-12-il-arasinda-d%c9%99yisir/)

### Inferences
- An MVP can be a small assignment ILP (OR-Tools/PuLP) over candidate standby buses and candidate insertion points, with deadhead time from a depot/stationing stop, a maximum lateness, and a hard driver shift-end constraint; it should output the top three options with costs rather than a single command.
- Validation without standby data: generate breakdowns at ~1 per 19,000 km (12,000 mi) and trip cancellations at an assumed 5–20% rate, replay on the calibrated line simulator, and report wait-time and denied-boarding differences between the ILP and a "nearest available bus" heuristic; label all of it scenario analysis.

### Gaps
- No Baku-specific figures found on standby-fleet size, depot locations, or breakdown rates (AYNA cites average fleet age 7 years for the five big operators and 12 for others, which affects failure rates but gives no rate).
- No field evaluation found of a reserve-dispatch optimiser's realised benefit versus dispatcher practice.

## Key Question 6: Simulation validity (calibrating a line simulator to AVL: link-time distributions, dwell models; the danger of idealised "−70% wait" claims; honest ranges; validation practice)

### Takeaway
Calibrated mesoscopic line simulators use lognormal (often shifted) link travel times, dwell times driven by headway-dependent boarding, and are validated by comparing simulated versus observed headway distributions with KS/Wilcoxon tests over many replications; even so, published pre-deployment benefits assumed ideal conditions, and the one RL field pilot delivered single-digit wait-time gains at 35–57% compliance. Present ranges conditioned on compliance, GPS quality and missing vehicles, never a single idealised number.

### Cited Findings
- Boston Route 1 calibration of Daganzo's bus-motion model from 60-s AVL: β (dwell sensitivity to headway) estimated by regression with the expected positive sign in 27 of 32 segments; Daganzo's typical β range 10^-2 to 1; gamma distributions fit headways; a lognormal noise term passed the KS test at 95% on all segments but the first — [Yang et al., TRR](https://humnetlab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/TRRecordsBuses.pdf)
- Hangzhou: link travel times fit to shifted lognormal distributions; bus-bay interactions modelled as a FIFO queue; link travel-time reliability identified as the main driver of path reliability — [Dai, Ma & Chen, J. ITS 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/15472450.2018.1470932)
- Toronto data-driven mesoscopic model: random-forest running-speed models plus lognormal dwell-time distributions "adequately replicated variation in headways, delays, and dwell times," but stop- and route-level validation "demonstrated the need to capture passenger demand and congestion variations" — [Wen 2017, U of T](http://hdl.handle.net/1807/79147)
- Brisbane Route 555: 4 months of AVL and smart-card data; five parameters calibrated with the Cross-Entropy Method, each candidate evaluated over 100 replications; two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests compared simulated and observed headway distributions at stops — [Kieu et al., Transportmetrica B 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/151530/1/Bus_simulation_paper__Transportmetrica_B___2019_%20%281%29.pdf)
- Portland TriMet forecasting framework: "only bus models accounting for the bus load can provide valid forecasts of a bus route over a large prediction horizon, especially for headway variations"; adding signal timings and traffic flows did not significantly improve prediction — [Andersson et al., TR-C 2015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0968090X15003794)
- Validation practice: compare simulated and real running time, headway and dwell with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over 1,000 runs; headways showed more rejections than dwell/run times — [PMC 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7205290/); CORSIM dwell module with dwell = f(arrival rate, boarding time, headway), calibrated with NJ Transit route 39 data — [Chien et al., J. Transp. Eng. 2000](https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290733-947X%282000%29126%3A5%28396%29)
- Common dwell parameters in holding simulations: boarding 3.0 s/pax, alighting 1.8 s/pax, Poisson arrivals, 1-s resolution — [Wang & Sun 2020](https://lijunsun.github.io/files/papers/2020-TRC-Bus.pdf)
- Idealisation warnings: "In the majority of the cases the benefits of these models have been estimated assuming ideal operational conditions while only few of them have been tested in real conditions" — [Phillips et al. 2015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856415001639); the simulated RL benchmark of 2026 states its findings "are limited to the simulated setting, as observed operating trajectories were unavailable for external calibration" — [Zhang et al. 2026](https://academic.oup.com/tse/advance-article/doi/10.1093/tse/tdag059/8812242)
- Field anchor for the sim-to-field gap: CTA's RL was trained with non-compliance sampled uniformly between 50% and 100% of the instructed hold, yet realised compliance was 35% (terminal) and 56.9% (en route) and realised wait-time gains were 5.7–8.7% (0.3–0.6 min) with 1.6 min in the PM peak; the authors say feedback "can also reduce the difference between simulated and real-world conditions" — [arXiv 2509.08231](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08231)
- Georgia Tech's headway control reduced the extreme-headway range (max 14:59 vs 19:45; min 2:28 vs 0:01) rather than producing a dramatic mean-wait collapse — [Bartholdi & Eisenstein](https://www2.isye.gatech.edu/~jjb/papers/Bartholdi-Eisenstein-2011.pdf)

### Inferences
- A credible demo shows three bars per metric: (a) ideal (100% compliance, 15-s GPS, all buses tracked), (b) CTA-like (50% compliance, 60-s GPS, 10% of buses untracked, 5-min lateness cap), (c) pessimistic (35% compliance concentrated in a few drivers, which Phillips shows hurts most). Report headway coefficient of variation and the share of headways <2 min or >2× scheduled, not only mean wait.
- Before any control result, show the baseline simulator reproduces observed headway distributions (KS p-values by stop) from AYNA AVL; without that step a "−70% wait" chart is the single most attackable slide.
- With the schedule-based status quo in Baku (fare-collection incentives, partial GPS), the realistic first-pilot claim is "fewer extreme gaps and bunches on one route," in the CTA 5–10% wait-time range.

### Gaps
- No Baku AVL dataset was found publicly; link-time and dwell distributions for Baku routes would have to come from AYNA or be scraped from the AYNA MaaS feed (legality unverified).

## Key Question 8: Privacy and ethics (driver monitoring/performance scoring via telematics; passenger location data; Azerbaijan's Law on Personal Data)

### Takeaway
Azerbaijan's Law on Personal Data (No. 998-IIIQ, 11 May 2010) requires written consent for processing and for any transfer to third parties, purpose limitation, state registration of personal-data information systems (employment-relations data exempt), and restricts cross-border transfer to countries with equivalent protection; legal commentary holds that employee consent is not freely given, so driver monitoring must rest on the employer's legal duties and cannot be covert. For a pilot this means Ritm processes driver GPS/compliance data only as the operator's contracted processor, avoids individual driver scoring, hosts data in-country, and uses only aggregated passenger counts.

### Cited Findings
- Confidential personal data may be given to third parties only with the subject's consent except as provided by law (Art. 5.2); open-category data are anonymised data or data the subject has made public (Art. 5.3) — [e-qanun consolidated text](https://frameworks.e-qanun.az/19/f_19675.html)
- Collection and processing require the subject's written consent, including as an electronic document with enhanced e-signature (Art. 8.1); consent must state the controller, purpose, list of data and operations, validity period and withdrawal terms, and destruction/archiving conditions (Art. 8.2) — [Mincom PDF of the law](https://mincom.gov.az/storage/pages/376/998-iiiq-ferdi-melumatlar-haqqinda.pdf)
- Purpose must be precisely declared; data collected only for lawful, pre-defined purposes; volume proportionate to purpose; data destroyed when the purpose is achieved (Art. 9.1–9.4); the controller must inform the subject of identity, purpose and legal basis, protection level, certification/state expertise status, intended users and rights (Art. 11.2); requests answered within 7 working days, extendable by 7 (Art. 12.4) — [idp.gov.az text](https://idp.gov.az/az/law/143/parent/15)
- Transfer of personal data to any third party requires the subject's written consent (Art. 13.1), except open-category data, transfers by state bodies performing their duties, or protection of life and health (Art. 13.2); cross-border transfer is prohibited where the destination country does not ensure protection at the level of Azerbaijani law (Art. 14.2.2) unless the subject consents (Art. 14.3) — [idp.gov.az](https://idp.gov.az/az/law/143/parent/15)
- Personal-data information systems "must undergo state registration" and collection without registration is not allowed (Art. 15.1–15.2); exemptions include systems holding data on persons in labour relations with the owner/operator and systems below a threshold set by the executive authority (Art. 15.3) — [ILO NATLEX English translation](https://natlex.ilo.org/dyn/natlex2/natlex2/files/download/86656/AZE-86656.pdf); registration takes up to one month — [globaldatarules](https://globaldatarules.com/country/AZ); DLA Piper: systems must be registered with the DPA; processing on consent or for open-category data; transfer needs prior written consent — [DLA Piper handbook (AZ)](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/guide.pdf?c=AZ)
- Workplace monitoring analysis (Azerbaijani law): because the employee is financially dependent, consent "in principle cannot be considered as freely given"; the only basis employers use is Labour Code Art. 222.1 (organising supervision of healthy and safe working conditions); covert surveillance, interception of private communications and open recording "despite their objection" are prohibited (Law on Freedom of Information Art. 14); employment-related systems are exempt from registration — [LHRI, Privacy and Workplace Monitoring](https://www.lhri.az/en/library-download/2172)
- Violations entail administrative liability under the Code of Administrative Offences — [Legal 500, Sep 2024](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/azerbaijan/privacy/data-protection-and-employee-privacy-in-azerbaijan)
- Driver-facing ethics evidence: CTA tracked non-compliance via supervisor logs and linked it to seniority; drivers feared holding would "cut into their break time"; the authors recommend training and incentives rather than enforcement alone — [arXiv 2509.08231](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08231); senior drivers in Santiago were more reluctant to use headway tools — [Transportmetrica A 2023 via citing page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856415001639)
- Passenger data: AYNA MaaS collects "Personal info and App activity," states "No data shared with third parties" — [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?hl=en_US&id=com.aynamaas.app); Wi-Fi probe counting of waiting passengers raises privacy concerns and is degraded by MAC randomisation — [Arai et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2102.01364)

### Inferences
- Driver GPS traces and compliance logs are employee personal data; the operator (BakuBus/private carrier) is the controller, Ritm at most an operator under a written contract (Art. 10.3). Public or per-driver leaderboards, or any "performance score" shared beyond the employer, would sit outside the safe-working-conditions basis and should be excluded from the pilot design.
- Keep all servers in Azerbaijan (Art. 14), process only aggregated stop-level counts for passengers, and expect to need AYNA's written data-sharing agreement before any real AVL leaves AYNA systems; the de-identification practice of DataRace is a local precedent to cite.

### Gaps
- No Azerbaijani regulation or guidance specific to vehicle telematics/GPS monitoring of employed drivers was found.
- The applicability of the registration exemption to a third-party (non-employer) processor holding driver GPS data is untested in the sources found.

## Key Question 9: Timeline realism (what a 4–5 person student team can build in 4–6 weeks; what to cut)

### Takeaway
Comparable systems took far longer to reach operation (Georgia Tech: >1 year of live trials before a 2012 demo, full-time use 2013; CTA: two pilots across 2022–2023 run by a university team), while recent Baku hackathon winners produced dashboards and a model on real data in 48 hours. In 4–6 weeks a student team can credibly deliver a calibrated line simulator, a transparent holding rule with guardrails and fallback, a tablet-style driver UI mock and a dispatcher view with honest scenario ranges; RL training, stop-level ML crowd forecasting without tap data, a full crew-aware rescheduling ILP and any in-cab validator integration should be cut or demoted to roadmap.

### Cited Findings
- Georgia Tech student VIP team: tablet-based system under "live trials for over one year," public demonstration 20 Apr 2012, "full-time use at Georgia Tech in August 2013" — [GT project page](https://www.isye.gatech.edu/~jjb/buses/buses.html); [EurekAlert, Apr 2012](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/913028); architecture paper, Sept 2012 — [IEEE SASO workshop](https://doi.org/10.1109/sasow.2012.20)
- CTA: route 81 pilot one week (17–21 Oct 2022), route 66 pilot two weeks (6–17 Nov 2023); DSS was a web app on supervisors' existing phones with a Northeastern research assistant monitoring remotely — [arXiv 2509.08231](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08231)
- MDOT MTA ran a 15-day Onboard App pilot on one division before deciding on wider rollout — [Mass Transit, Mar 2026](https://www.masstransitmag.com/technology/news/55362287/mdot-mta-aims-to-improve-on-time-performance-by-empowering-bus-operators-with-real-time-information)
- Swiftly (VENDOR) says its operator app needs "no integrations with other onboard systems" and runs on off-the-shelf Android tablets — [Swiftly](https://www.goswift.ly/blog/introducing-swiftlys-onboard-app)
- Metro Hackathon winners built two working dashboards plus video analytics on real metro data in ~48 hours (self-reported) — [LinkedIn](https://linkedin.com/in/telmanhaji); TransitHack 2024 gave teams one extra development week before the event — [transithack.az](https://transithack.az/)
- Post-event activity falls to 17% of projects by day 6 — [Nolte et al. 2020](https://alexandernolte.github.io/download/Nolte%20et%20al.%20(2020)%20What%20Happens%20to%20All%20These%20Hackathon%20Projects%20-%20Identifying%20Factors%20to%20Promote%20Hackathon%20Project%20Continuation.pdf)
- A one-parameter headway rule needs only "the arrival times of the current bus and the preceding bus relative to the virtual schedule" — [Xuan et al. 2011](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0jp7c8k8/qt0jp7c8k8.pdf); the Bartholdi rule needs only the estimated time until the next bus arrives — [GT page](https://www.isye.gatech.edu/~jjb/buses/buses.html)

### Inferences
- Feasible in 4–6 weeks (inference from the above): (1) ingest one route's AVL (from AYNA under agreement, or scraped/synthetic with provenance stated); (2) event-based simulator with lognormal link times and headway-dependent dwell, validated by KS tests against observed headways; (3) two-way-looking holding rule, α≈0.5 backward / α<0.5 two-way, 30-s steps, 60–120-s cap, 5-min lateness guard, forward/schedule fallback when the follower is untracked; (4) Android/web "hold N s" driver screen with a countdown; (5) dispatcher ladder view with data-quality flags; (6) a toy reserve-assignment ILP over 2–3 standby buses with deadhead and shift-end constraints; (7) a results slide with ideal/CTA-like/pessimistic ranges.
- Cut or label as roadmap: RL controller (keep as offline experiment only), stop-level ML crowd forecast without tap data (replace with headway-based load proxy), crew-integrated rescheduling, any claim of validator/MDT integration, and any single-number benefit claim above ~10–15% wait reduction.

### Gaps
- No source directly measures student-team build time for a holding-control prototype; the 4–6-week scope is an inference from the component simplicity shown in the control papers and the hackathon outputs above.
